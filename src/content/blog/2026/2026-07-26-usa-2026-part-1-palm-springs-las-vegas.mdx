This is part 1 of our USA 2026 trip.

Not 16 days, as I first wrote. **23 days.**

That matters, because 23 days in the States with small children is not one trip. It is a series of smaller trips wearing the same suitcase.

Part 1 is the landing. **LAX, Palm Springs, Las Vegas**, and the part where everyone pretends jet lag is not quietly driving the family bus.

The first rule of travelling to the US with children is that the children do not care about your route.

They do not care that you landed in **LAX**.

They do not care that the itinerary has desert, cities, road-trip energy, and an amount of driving that would make a Danish person check the map twice and then sit down.

They care about three things:

- is there a pool
- is there food
- why are we walking

Honestly, fair.

## LAX was not the destination

We started the trip the only sensible way: by landing in Los Angeles and immediately leaving Los Angeles.

Not because LA is bad.

LA is probably great if you have sleep, patience, and the emotional stamina required to treat traffic as a lifestyle.

We had children and luggage.

So the real opening move was simple: get out, get east, get to **Palm Springs**, and let the desert villa do what airports cannot.

![The Palm Springs pool doing exactly what a first stop after LAX is supposed to do: lower everyone's blood pressure](/images/usa-2026/IMG_0883.jpg)

## Palm Springs fixed the landing

Palm Springs was the correct first stop.

After a long flight, the goal is not culture. The goal is survival with nicer lighting.

The Airbnb villa gave us the three things every family trip needs after crossing the Atlantic:

- water
- shade
- enough physical space that nobody has to negotiate every single square meter

It looked calm from the outside.

Inside, of course, it was still us.

There is no version of "private villa" that turns children into chilled resort people. They still need snacks. They still ask where everything is. They still behave like the pool is a legal obligation rather than a luxury.

But as first stops go, Palm Springs did its job beautifully.

It let us arrive properly.

Not elegantly. Let us not get carried away.

But properly.

## Las Vegas remained Las Vegas

From there we went to **Las Vegas**, which is not so much a city as a dare with hotel rooms.

Vegas with children is strange.

Everything is too big, too bright, too loud, too fake, and somehow still exactly what it says on the box.

Adults stand there thinking, "What is this place?"

Children stand there thinking, "Why does every building look like someone was allowed to choose one theme and chose all of them?"

The city has no indoor voice.

That is probably the point.

![Las Vegas doing Las Vegas things: too much scale, too much shine, and somehow still entertaining](/images/usa-2026/IMG_0896.jpg)

## Bubba Gump was the correct kind of wrong

The proper cultural stop, obviously, was **Bubba Gump**.

Not because it is fine dining.

Because sometimes family travel is not about optimal local cuisine. Sometimes it is about sitting down, eating something predictable, and letting the adults pretend they chose the chaos on purpose.

There is a time for discovering hidden restaurants.

There is also a time for accepting that shrimp, fries, air-conditioning, and a chair can become a complete spiritual framework.

This was that time.

![Bubba Gump, because every carefully planned road trip eventually becomes a shrimp-based negotiation](/images/usa-2026/IMG_0890.jpg)

## The arrival tax is real

That was the main lesson from the first leg.

Every big family trip has an arrival tax.

You pay it in tired adults, confused children, bags in the wrong corner of the room, snack logistics, and at least one moment where everyone has a slightly different definition of "almost there."

Palm Springs helped.

Las Vegas tested us.

Bubba Gump stabilized the system.

Not glamorous, maybe.

But real.

And that is the point of this kind of trip. You do not get the clean travel-magazine version. You get the version with pool towels, sunscreen, weird timing, and children who can turn any plan into a live negotiation.

Part 1 was not the big scenery chapter.

It was the "get the family across the ocean and functioning again" chapter.

Which, frankly, is underrated.
